Learn the Pigeonhole Principle

A small counting rule with surprisingly powerful consequences.

The idea

What is the Pigeonhole Principle?

If you put more objects into fewer boxes, at least one box must contain more than one object. The objects can be anything and the boxes can represent any categories, locations, or choices.

It is not a rule about pigeons alone. Pigeons and holes are simply an easy picture for a general fact about counting: when there are not enough places to keep everything separate, repetition is unavoidable.

Formal definition

The principle

If n + 1 objects are placed into n boxes, then at least one box contains at least two objects.

n + 1 objects → n boxes → one box has ≥ 2 objects

With 3 pigeons and only 2 holes, the first two pigeons could use different holes, but the third pigeon has nowhere new to go. It must share a hole.

The generalized principle

More objects, more boxes

If N objects are distributed among k boxes, at least one box contains at least:

ceil(N / k)

For example, distributing 17 objects among 5 boxes guarantees that one box contains at least ceil(17 / 5) = 4 objects.

Reasoning

Why does it work?

Imagine trying to keep every box below the claimed number. If each of the k boxes held fewer than ceil(N / k) objects, the total number of objects would be too small to reach N. Since all N objects must go somewhere, at least one box reaches that ceiling.

Quick example

13 students, 12 birth months

  • Objects: 13 students
  • Boxes: 12 months
  • Conclusion: at least 2 students share a birth month
Try this example

Could every student have a different birth month? No. There are only 12 months for 13 students, so the thirteenth student must share a month with someone else.
